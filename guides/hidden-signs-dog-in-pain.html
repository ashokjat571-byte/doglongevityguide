The Hidden Signs Your Dog Is in Pain (That Owners Miss)

Dogs are descended from animals that survived by not advertising weakness, and they still hide pain brilliantly. By the time an owner notices limping, the pain is usually old. The earlier signs are quieter — and they are the ones that, caught in time, save months of comfort.

The short version: Watch for changes, not events: slower mornings, reluctance on stairs or jumps, licking or chewing at a spot, panting at rest, yawning or lip-licking when nothing is funny, sleeping more, withdrawing from family, or new grumpiness when touched. Any of these lasting more than a few days deserves a vet check — pain is the most under-treated condition in senior dogs, and it is also one of the most treatable.

The quiet signals, grouped

Movement

  • Slower to rise, especially after rest ("morning stiffness" that improves as the day warms up is classic arthritis).
  • Bunny-hopping, short steps, or sitting down on walks.
  • Avoiding stairs, cars, jumps — or hesitating at thresholds.

Behaviour

  • Withdrawing from play, family or favourite people; hiding more.
  • New irritability: growling or snapping when touched in a spot that never bothered them.
  • Restlessness at night, or sleeping far more than usual.

Body language

  • Excessive licking or chewing at joints, paws or belly (dogs lick what hurts).
  • Panting, yawning or lip-licking at rest — stress signals, not tiredness.
  • A tucked tail, hunched back, or ears held flat for long periods.
  • Eating more slowly, dropping food, or preferring soft food (mouth pain is silent too).

“Old age” is not a diagnosis

The most dangerous sentence in senior care is "he's just old." Old age explains slower; it does not explain pain. Arthritis, dental disease, ear infections, urinary pain and cancers all hide behind that phrase — and most have treatments that restore comfort within days to weeks.

What to do

  • Keep a 2-minute log for a week: what changed, when, and what helps or worsens it. Vets diagnose from patterns.
  • Film it. A 30-second video of your dog rising or walking beats any description at the clinic — dogs often "perform" better at the vet's.
  • Ask specifically: "Could this be pain?" — and ask about a pain-relief trial: if a short, vet-supervised trial transforms your dog, you have your answer.

Your dog cannot ask for help. The job of noticing belongs to you — and the reward for noticing is often a dog who suddenly seems years younger.
