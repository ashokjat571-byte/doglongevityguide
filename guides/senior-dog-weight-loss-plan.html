How to Help an Overweight Senior Dog Lose Weight Safely

Half or more of pet dogs in the US and UK carry excess weight — and in seniors the stakes are highest, because fat loads arthritic joints, strains hearts and shortens life. The good news: the most powerful longevity intervention in dog science is a measuring cup. The bad news: done badly, weight loss in an old dog can do real harm. Here is the safe way.

The short version: Agree a target weight and a daily calorie allowance with your vet first (usually based on resting energy requirement for the target weight, not the current one). Aim for slow loss — roughly 1–2% of body weight per week at most — weigh monthly, keep protein up to protect muscle, and move daily in short bouts. Never starve a senior dog.

Step 1 — the vet conversation first

Before cutting food, rule out the look-alikes: hypothyroidism (very treatable, common in seniors), Cushing's disease, and pain that has quietly cut activity. Your vet will also set the target weight and check whether a therapeutic weight-loss diet or a standard senior diet suits your dog's kidneys and other conditions.

Step 2 — count, then cut gently

  • Weigh food, every meal. A kitchen scale beats any scoop. Most "diet failures" are measurement failures.
  • Count treats in the budget. Treats and chews should total under ~10% of daily calories; swap biscuits for green beans, cucumber or a fraction of the daily kibble as training rewards.
  • Cut in small steps (typically 10–20% below current intake, per your vet) and hold for 2–4 weeks before judging — weight loss lags.
  • Split the ration into 2–3 meals; it steadies hunger and blood sugar.

Step 3 — move to protect muscle

Fat loss without movement costs muscle — exactly what a senior cannot spare. Short, frequent walks, gentle hills, sit-to-stand games and (where available) swimming or hydrotherapy keep the loss fat, not muscle. If your dog hurts, treat the pain first — a comfortable dog moves, and a moving dog loses weight.

What NOT to do

  • No crash diets. Severe restriction risks muscle wasting, gallbladder problems and, in dogs with other diseases, dangerous complications.
  • No human diet foods. "Light" human products, xylitol-sweetened items and fad diets have no place in a dog's bowl.
  • No guessing. If weight stalls for 6+ weeks, or your dog loses appetite, vomits, or seems dull, call your vet — plateau and illness look identical from the outside.

The target is not a number on a chart — it is a dog who can walk further, breathe easier and play a little longer. In the famous lifetime study, that was worth a median 1.8 years. Your measuring cup is the cheapest longevity drug that exists.
