Cognitive Decline in Dogs: Early Signs Most Owners Miss

An old dog standing in a corner, staring. A dog who has started pacing at 3 a.m., or who no longer greets you at the door. Owners usually call it "just getting old." Veterinarians increasingly call it what it is: canine cognitive dysfunction (CCD) — the dog version of dementia — and studies suggest it affects a large share of dogs over 11, and a majority over 15, while remaining heavily underdiagnosed.

The short version: Watch for the DISHAA signs below, especially changes in sleep, orientation and interaction. CCD cannot be cured, but early action — vet workup, routine, mental enrichment, diet and sleep support — can slow it and dramatically improve an old dog's nights and days.

The DISHAA checklist

  • Disorientation — stuck in corners, going to the wrong side of the door, staring at walls.
  • Interaction changes — clingier or more distant, less greeting, less play.
  • Sleep–wake changes — restless nights, vocalizing after dark, sleeping all day.
  • House-soiling — a housetrained dog having accidents, or forgetting to signal.
  • Activity changes — repetitive pacing or licking, or losing interest in everything.
  • Anxiety — new fears, panting, distress when routines change or at nightfall ("sundowning").

First: rule out look-alikes

Pain (especially arthritis), failing eyesight or hearing, thyroid disease, high blood pressure and brain or liver problems can all imitate dementia. A senior workup — exam, bloodwork, blood pressure — comes before any CCD conclusion. This matters because the look-alikes are often treatable.

What genuinely helps

  • Brain work, daily. In Dog Aging Project data, dogs active for an hour or more a day had far lower odds of CCD signs. Sniff walks, puzzle feeders, gentle training games — the aging brain responds to use.
  • Protect the night. A consistent evening routine, a night light, a calm wind-down walk, and white noise help many sundowners. Tell your vet about night waking — there are prescription and supplement options with evidence behind them.
  • Diet. Antioxidant-rich senior diets and MCT (medium-chain triglyceride) foods have clinical evidence for improving cognitive signs in some dogs.
  • Make the house easy. Ramps, non-slip runners, baby gates at stairs, food and water in predictable places — reducing confusion reduces anxiety.

If your old dog is "not himself," do not accept it as inevitable aging until a vet has checked. Confusion is a symptom, not a personality — and often, something can be done.
