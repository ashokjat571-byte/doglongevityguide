Dog Cancer Signs in Seniors: Early Warning Signs Owners Miss

Cancer causes nearly half of deaths in dogs over 10 — and it is also the disease where owners most often say, “I wish I'd come sooner.” Early cancer is usually silent and painless, which is why it is missed. The signs below are not diagnoses; they are prompts. Most turn out benign. The ones that aren't are the ones early checking catches.

Key takeaways:
  • Any new lump, any lump that grows or changes, and any unexplained weight loss in a senior dog deserves a vet check — most lumps are benign, but only testing tells.
  • Warning signs beyond lumps: sores that don't heal, bleeding, difficulty eating or swallowing, persistent lameness, and behavior change.
  • Early detection changes options: surgery cures some cancers outright when caught small.
  • Map your dog's lumps monthly (our workbook has a lump map) and photograph changes.

The signs, grouped

Lumps and bumps

Feel your dog monthly: belly, chest, legs, neck, mouth. Note size (compare to a coin), firmness, mobility (does it move under the skin?), and growth speed. Fast-growing, fixed, or ulcerated lumps move up the priority list. Lipomas (fatty lumps) are common and usually harmless — but a new lump is never “just a lipoma” until checked, because mast cell tumors can feel identical.

Weight, appetite and energy

Unexplained weight loss, reduced appetite, or new tiredness in a senior dog are cancer prompts until proven otherwise — alongside kidney disease and dental pain (see our weight-loss guide). Cancer often announces itself through the whole body before any lump appears.

Bleeding and wounds

Nosebleeds, blood in urine or stool, bleeding from the mouth, or a sore that doesn't heal in 2 weeks all deserve prompt investigation. Hemangiosarcoma (spleen/heart) can present dramatically — sudden collapse and pale gums are an emergency.

Eating, breathing, moving

Difficulty swallowing or chewing (mouth tumors), persistent cough or breathing change (lung spread or primary lung disease), and lameness that doesn't fit an injury (bone cancer, especially in large breeds) are all senior-cancer presentations vets take seriously.

What the vet check involves

Usually: exam, fine-needle aspirate (a quick, usually painless needle sample examined under microscope — this distinguishes most lump types), bloodwork, and X-rays or ultrasound if spread is a question. Aspiration is cheap, fast, and the single most underused early-detection tool in senior care.

Frequently asked questions

Are all lumps cancer?

No — most are benign (lipomas, cysts, warts). But “most” is not a diagnosis for your dog's lump. Aspiration is how you find out, and it takes minutes.

Which breeds face the highest cancer risk?

Golden Retrievers, Boxers, Bernese Mountain Dogs, Rottweilers and large breeds generally face higher rates; hemangiosarcoma, lymphoma and osteosarcoma dominate. Breed risk means earlier vigilance, not fatalism.

Is treatment worthwhile in an old dog?

Often, yes — surgery for a localised tumor, and some chemotherapies, are well tolerated by seniors, with quality of life as the deciding measure. Age alone should never be the reason to decline a consultation; the oncologist's assessment should be.

The best cancer tool you own is your hands, once a month, and the habit of acting on change. Early is everything — and early is free.
