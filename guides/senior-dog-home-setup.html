Setting Up Your Home for a Senior Dog: Ramps, Mats and Beds

The most effective senior-dog intervention available costs less than a vet visit: changing the house to fit the dog you have now, not the puppy you had. Falls, slips and failed jumps cause more senior injuries than almost anything else — and they are preventable with a weekend of adjustments.

Key takeaways:
  • Priority order: non-slip surfaces first, then ramps, then bed, then lighting — in that order of injury prevention.
  • Ramps beat stairs for cars, sofas and beds; steps are a second choice for dogs who refuse ramps.
  • An orthopedic bed is not a luxury for an arthritic dog — it is joint care.
  • Keep food, water, toilet access and your dog's bed within easy, step-free reach, including at night.

Floors: the number-one fix

Hard floors are ice rinks for old dogs with weak hind legs. Fix them with: runners along the routes your dog actually uses (bed to door, bed to bowl), non-slip mats at food/water and doorways, and rugs with grippy backing (not loose rugs that slide). Nail trims and paw-hair trims between the toes also restore grip — ask your groomer or vet to show you.

Ramps and access

  • Car: a ramp or a step platform — jumping down from a car boot is a classic cruciate and spine injury in seniors.
  • Sofa/bed: a ramp (gentle slope, non-slip surface) or pet stairs; train with treats over a few days rather than expecting instant use.
  • Garden steps: a ramp alongside steps, or a temporary plywood ramp with outdoor carpet for grip.
  • Thresholds: small ramps or beveled strips over raised door thresholds that trip stiff dogs.

Bed, bowls and light

  • Bed: thick orthopedic foam (not a thin pad on the floor), raised slightly off cold floors, in a draft-free spot near you. Older dogs sleep 12–18 hours — the bed is where they live.
  • Bowls: raised to elbow height for arthritic necks and backs, non-slip base, and a mat underneath for spills.
  • Lighting: night lights along the route to the door for dogs with failing eyesight; avoid moving furniture (blind and deaf dogs navigate by memory).
  • Toilet access: a nearby night-time option (pad or easy garden access) prevents accidents and midnight distress.

Room by room

  • Kitchen: mats, raised bowls, secure bins (old dogs counter-surf less but scavenge more when hungry or confused).
  • Living room: ramp to the sofa if they sleep there, a bed beside it as an alternative, runners on the main path.
  • Bedroom: bed on the floor or a ramp to your bed; night light; water bowl nearby.
  • Bathroom/grooming: non-slip mat in the bath, a shower attachment (easier than lifting into a tub), and a drying routine that doesn't chill them.

Frequently asked questions

Will my dog actually use a ramp?

Most do with a week of treat-based training: start flat on the floor with treats, then raise gradually. Dogs who refuse ramps often accept wide, low steps instead.

Are orthopedic beds worth the price?

For an arthritic senior, yes — thick memory foam distributes weight and reduces pressure pain, and owners consistently report better sleep and easier mornings. It is the cheapest joint intervention after weight control.

What about stair gates?

Use them: a fall down stairs can end a senior dog's mobility. Gate the stairs and give your dog everything they need on one level.

Your house got your dog through puppyhood. A few mats, a ramp and a better bed will get them through old age — the best return on investment in senior care.
