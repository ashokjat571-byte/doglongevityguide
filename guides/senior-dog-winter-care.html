Winter Care for Senior Dogs: Cold, Joints and Safety

Winter is the hardest season for an old dog. Cold stiffens arthritic joints, chills thin bodies, dries skin, and turns a simple walk into a slip hazard. With a little planning, though, winter can be the season your senior dog is most comfortable — if you adjust the routine to the weather.

The short version: Keep senior dogs warm (coats for short-coated, lean and very old dogs), shorten walks but don't skip them, protect paws from ice and road salt, watch for stiffness that worsens in the cold, and never leave an old dog outside or in a cold car. Hypothermia risk rises sharply in thin, very old and arthritic dogs.

Why cold hits seniors hardest

  • Arthritis flares in the cold. Stiff mornings get stiffer; warm-up time doubles. This is pain, not mood — and it responds to pain control, warmth and gentle movement.
  • Less fat, less muscle, less heat. Lean seniors and very old dogs lose body heat fast and cannot shiver it back as effectively.
  • Slippery = dangerous. One fall on ice can fracture a fragile senior or end their confidence in walking.
  • Dry air, dry skin. Central heating dries skin and noses; cracked pads and itchy skin follow.

The winter routine

  • Coat up when it counts: below ~7°C (45°F), most seniors benefit from a coat; short-coated, small and very old dogs benefit sooner. It should cover chest and belly without restricting movement.
  • Shorter, more frequent walks: three 10-minute outings beat one 30-minute march in freezing weather — same total movement, far less chilling.
  • Paws: rinse after walks to remove road salt (which irritates and is toxic if licked), dry between toes, and use paw balm or booties on icy ground.
  • Warm bed, warm house: a raised bed off cold floors, out of drafts, near (not on) a heat source. Heated beds must be pet-safe and thermostat-controlled.
  • Keep moving indoors: on brutal days, hallway fetch, gentle stair-free games and sniff puzzles maintain muscle without the freeze.
  • Watch water and weight: indoor dogs drink less and move less in winter; check intake and weigh monthly so winter weight doesn't quietly accumulate.

Warning signs — get warm and call the vet

  • Violent shivering that stops suddenly, weakness, or collapse (late hypothermia signs — emergency).
  • Limping or lameness that appears only in cold weather and persists indoors.
  • Pale or blue gums, or a dog who is unresponsive to calling.

Winter care is mostly about respect: respect the cold, respect the joints, respect the thinner margin of an old body — and your senior dog can enjoy the season as much as any puppy, just in a coat, on a shorter walk, back to a warm bed.
