“My Dog Is 15”: What Owners of Very Old Dogs Do Differently

Fifteen is an age most dogs never reach, and it deserves celebration. It is also a different phase of care: the goal shifts from adding years to protecting comfort, dignity and the small daily pleasures your dog still loves.

The short version: Past 15, prioritise pain control, easy access to everything (food, water, toilet, bed, you), sleep protection, and a written quality-of-life plan made with your vet while you are calm. Watch for the emergencies that move fast in very old dogs, and judge days by good moments, not by comparisons with their younger self.

What changes at 15

  • Everything is more fragile. Kidneys, heart, liver and joints have little reserve; a minor infection or a missed meal can tip a very old dog into crisis — and recovery is slower.
  • Senses fade. Deafness and failing eyesight are common; use touch, scent and consistent routines, and keep furniture exactly where it is.
  • Muscle and weight melt fast. A 15-year-old can lose condition in weeks. Weigh regularly, feed for palatability and protein (with your vet), and treat appetite loss as a prompt to investigate, not a quirk.
  • Sleep dominates. Very old dogs may sleep 18–20 hours a day. That is normal — protect it, and watch the night for restlessness and sundowning (see our dementia guide).

The comfort checklist

  • Pain first: if your dog is stiff, reluctant or withdrawn, assume pain until your vet says otherwise — modern pain relief transforms very old dogs.
  • Ramps everywhere: beds, sofas, cars and steps. One bad fall can end a good run.
  • Non-slip everything: runners on hard floors, mats by food and water.
  • Toilet access: frequent trips, a nearby night-time option, and no shame about accidents.
  • Warmth: old dogs chill easily; a warm bed out of drafts is medicine.

Quality of life, honestly assessed

Vets often suggest scoring five things weekly — eating, drinking, moving, toileting and joy (does your dog still greet you, enjoy a sniff, wag at a favourite person?). More good days than bad, with comfort maintained, is the goal. When bad days consistently outnumber good ones, or pain cannot be controlled, a gentle conversation with your vet about end-of-life timing is kindness, not failure — and deciding while you are calm is far kinder than deciding in a crisis.

Fifteen years of love is a rare gift. The last chapter deserves the same care as the first: attention, comfort, and presence.
