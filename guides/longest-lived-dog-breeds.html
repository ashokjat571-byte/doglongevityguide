The Longest-Lived Dog Breeds: Who Actually Reaches 16, 17, 18?

Some dogs seem to defy the calendar. While giant breeds are lost at 7 or 8, a handful of breeds routinely celebrate 16th, 17th and even 18th birthdays. If longevity matters to you — whether choosing a breed or simply marvelling — here is who lives longest, and why.

The short version: The longest-lived breeds are almost all small: Chihuahuas, Toy Poodles, Jack Russell Terriers, Dachshunds, Shih Tzus, Lhasa Apsos, Yorkshire Terriers, Maltese, Pomeranians and Australian Cattle Dogs (the famous exception — a medium herding breed with exceptional longevity records). Common threads: small size, lower IGF-1, lean bodies, and owners who keep them active into old age.

The longevity leaderboard

  • Chihuahua — typically 14–17 years, with many reaching 18+. The classic small-dog advantage: tiny body, slow aging, big personality.
  • Toy Poodle — 14–18 years. Watch teeth (tiny mouths, big dental problems) and eyes, but the breed's lifespan is outstanding.
  • Jack Russell Terrier — 13–16 years, and famously youthful into old age. Their secret is partly temperament: they never stop moving.
  • Dachshund — 12–16 years. The caveat is the back: intervertebral disc disease is the breed's weak point, so weight control and ramp use are longevity care here.
  • Shih Tzu & Lhasa Apso — 13–16 years each; watch eyes and breathing (flat faces), but both age slowly.
  • Australian Cattle Dog — 12–16 years, with the most famous longevity anecdote in dogdom (Bluey, 29 years, unverified by modern records but emblematic). A working breed that stays fit by nature.
  • Yorkshire Terrier, Maltese, Pomeranian — typically 13–16 years, with dental care as the make-or-break habit.

What the long-lived have in common

  • Small size and lower IGF-1 — the growth-hormone story that explains why big dogs die younger works in reverse here.
  • Lean bodies: the breeds' owners, on average, keep them slimmer — and in the landmark Labrador study, leanness alone was worth a median 1.8 years.
  • Fewer breed-specific killers: most long-lived breeds lack a single dominant fatal disease (unlike, say, hemangiosarcoma in Goldens or bone cancer in giant breeds).
  • Active old age: terriers and herders keep moving, and movement protects muscle, joints and brain.

The honest caveat

Breed averages describe populations, not your dog. A lean, well-cared-for Labrador can outlive a neglected Chihuahua by years. Longevity is a recipe — genes set the range, and weight, teeth, screening and movement decide where inside that range your dog lands.
