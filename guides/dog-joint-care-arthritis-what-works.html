Joint Care That Works vs. Joint Care That Just Costs Money

Arthritis is the most common chronic disease of old dogs — by some estimates it affects the majority of dogs over 8 — and the market around it is enormous and uneven. Expensive powders, miracle chews and miracle beds compete for the same wallet. This is our honest ranking, built on evidence rather than marketing.

The short version: The proven core of arthritis care is: (1) get excess weight off, (2) omega-3s (EPA/DHA) at a real dose, (3) proper veterinary pain relief when needed, and (4) keep the dog moving with controlled, daily exercise and simple home changes. Most other products are optional extras with weak or mixed evidence.

Tier 1 — works, start here

  • Weight loss. In studies of overweight arthritic dogs, losing even ~6–9% of body weight visibly improved lameness. Fat also produces inflammatory chemicals — a lean dog hurts less, twice over.
  • Vet-prescribed pain relief. NSAIDs and newer options (including monthly anti-NGF injections where available) transform quality of life for many dogs. They require bloodwork and a vet who knows your dog — that is a feature, not a barrier.
  • Daily controlled movement. Little and often beats weekend marathons: several short, steady walks maintain muscle, and muscle is the joint's shock absorber.
  • Home engineering. Ramps for car and sofa, non-slip mats on hard floors, a supportive bed out of drafts, raised bowls for neck/shoulder pain. Cheap, immediate, permanent.

Tier 2 — reasonable evidence, worth discussing

  • Fish oil (EPA/DHA) — the best-studied supplement in this space, with clinical trials showing improved weight-bearing in arthritic dogs at proper doses. Dose matters more than brand.
  • Physiotherapy / hydrotherapy — supervised exercise builds muscle without pounding joints; good clinics also teach you a home routine.
  • Green-lipped mussel, undenatured collagen (UC-II) — some positive studies, smaller and weaker than the fish-oil evidence.

Tier 3 — mostly costs money

  • Generic "joint chews" with sprinkle-level doses of glucosamine — glucosamine/chondroitin evidence in dogs is inconsistent at best, and most chews are underdosed treats.
  • Magnetic collars, copper bracelets and similar devices — no credible evidence.
  • Any product promising to "regrow cartilage" or replace vet care — a claim no honest seller can currently make.

Spend first on the scale, the fish oil, the ramp and the vet. That order — boring as it sounds — is what the evidence supports, and it is where an old dog actually feels the difference.
