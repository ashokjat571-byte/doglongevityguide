Why Big Dogs Die Younger: The IGF-1 Story Every Large-Breed Owner Should Know

In almost every mammal species, bigger means longer-lived — elephants outlive mice many times over. Dogs break the rule spectacularly. Within this one species, the biggest dogs die youngest: a Bernese Mountain Dog averages 6–8 years and a Great Dane 7–10, while Chihuahuas and Toy Poodles routinely reach 14–18.

The short version: Large breeds were bred for size, and size is driven by a growth hormone called IGF-1. High IGF-1 doesn't just make a puppy grow fast and big — research links it to faster aging across the whole body. That is why biotech companies are now developing drugs that lower IGF-1 in big dogs.

It starts with how we bred them

Dog breeds are recent human creations, produced by intense selection for traits like size. Studies comparing breeds find that larger breeds have markedly higher blood levels of IGF-1 (insulin-like growth factor 1) than small breeds. Growth is useful in a young animal; the problem is that the same signal keeps running in adulthood, and in study after study — in worms, mice and dogs — lower IGF-1 signalling is associated with longer life.

Big dogs don't just die younger — they age faster

Large breeds reach senior status by age 7–8, develop age-related diseases earlier, and have especially high rates of cancers such as osteosarcoma (bone cancer). Their bodies quite literally run the aging program at higher speed. This is why veterinarians treat a 7-year-old Great Dane as a senior patient, while a 7-year-old Jack Russell is middle-aged.

The drugs being built for exactly this problem

Loyal, the dog-longevity biotech company, was founded on this insight. Its candidates LOY-001 (a long-acting injection) and LOY-003 (a daily pill) are designed to reduce IGF-1 in large and giant breeds, with the goal of giving big dogs some of the longevity small dogs enjoy naturally. The FDA has accepted the effectiveness data package for LOY-003 — a regulatory first for a drug aimed at lifespan itself. Neither drug is on sale yet.

What a large-breed owner can do today

  • Keep them lean. The landmark lifetime study in Labradors found lean dogs lived a median 1.8 years longer. In a giant breed, extra weight is even costlier.
  • Start senior screening early — from age 6–7 for giant breeds, including bloodwork and checking every new lump promptly.
  • Protect the joints: ramps over repeated jumps, controlled exercise, and maintaining muscle.
  • Feed for steady growth in puppyhood — overfeeding a giant-breed puppy is linked to skeletal disease.

Big dogs give us fewer years. The science now explains why — and, for the first time, is building tools to change it.
