Why Your Senior Dog Sleeps Differently — and When to Worry

Puppies sleep like the dead and nobody worries. Old dogs do the same — yet a change in sleep is one of the first things owners report, and one of the most useful diagnostic clues a vet can get. The trick is knowing which changes are aging and which are alarms.

The short version: Senior dogs normally sleep 12–18 hours a day (very old dogs even more), in shorter, lighter bouts. Worry when sleep changes suddenly or comes with other signs: restlessness and pacing at night, vocalising after dark, sleeping all day AND being awake all night, or a dog who can no longer settle. Those patterns point to pain, cognitive decline, anxiety or medical problems — all worth a vet visit.

What normal senior sleep looks like

  • More total sleep, spread through the day — long naps plus a long night, with light dozing between.
  • Deeper-looking naps but lighter night sleep: older dogs wake more easily and may potter at 3 a.m. and settle again.
  • Daytime dozing in sunspots and favourite beds — still responsive, still interested in food and walks after waking.

Sundowning: the pattern to recognise

Some dogs with cognitive decline (canine dementia) are fine by day and confused after dark: pacing, staring, getting stuck in corners, barking or whining at night, and sleeping all day. This is called sundowning, and it is one of the DISHAA signs in our dementia guide. It responds — sometimes dramatically — to a consistent evening routine, a night light, a late toilet trip, and vet-prescribed sleep or anxiety support.

When sleep change means "call the vet"

  • Sudden change over days, not months.
  • Night waking combined with panting, restlessness or distress (pain often shows at night, when distractions are gone).
  • Sleeping far more AND eating, drinking or toileting differently.
  • New snoring with pauses in breathing, or coughing that wakes the dog (heart and airway disease can present this way).

Protecting the night

  • Same routine, same times — last walk, toilet, bed, in the same order every night.
  • A comfortable, warm, draft-free bed in a quiet spot, with a night light on the route to the door for dogs with failing eyesight.
  • Daytime activity — a dog who moves and thinks by day sleeps better by night (short walks, sniff games, puzzle feeders).
  • Evening wind-down, not excitement: calm play and gentle handling in the last hour.

Sleep is where an old body repairs itself. Guard it like the health resource it is — and treat a changed night as information, not inconvenience.
