Do Old Dogs Still Need Vaccines? Senior Vaccination Explained

"He's old — does he really still need shots?" It is one of the most common senior-dog questions, and the answer is more interesting than a simple yes: core protection still matters at every age, but the schedule for an old dog should be personal, not automatic.

The short version: Senior dogs still need core vaccines (distemper, parvovirus, adenovirus) and rabies where legally required — immunity does not expire with age, and old dogs fight infection worse. What changes is the approach: your vet may space non-core vaccines by risk, use titre testing for some core vaccines, and weigh any vaccine against your dog's current health. Never skip rabies where the law requires it.

Core vs non-core, in senior terms

  • Core (nearly all dogs): canine distemper, parvovirus and adenovirus. Immunity from these often lasts years — which is why many vets now vaccinate core vaccines every 3 years rather than annually in adult dogs, including seniors.
  • Rabies: legally required in most US states and in Canada (and for travel everywhere); the UK is rabies-free but requires vaccination for dogs entering the country. This one is not optional where the law applies, whatever the dog's age.
  • Non-core (by risk and lifestyle): leptospirosis (very common in wet/rural areas and a genuine senior risk), kennel cough (Bordetella), Lyme disease (tick areas), canine influenza. An old dog who no longer boards or hikes may need fewer of these; a social, rural senior may need more.

Titre testing: the middle path

A titre test measures antibodies in the blood for distemper and parvovirus. If titres are protective, many vets will defer that vaccine — useful for owners worried about over-vaccinating a frail senior. Limits: titres don't exist for every disease (notably not for rabies in most legal frameworks), and a low titre doesn't automatically mean "vaccinate now" — it means discuss.

What changes with age

  • Immune response weakens (immunosenescence): vaccines may protect slightly less well in very old dogs, which is one reason senior boosters still matter.
  • Timing around illness: vets usually defer non-urgent vaccines during acute illness or flare-ups, and may split vaccines across visits for fragile seniors to reduce reaction risk.
  • Reactions: most are mild (soreness, a day of sleepiness). Severe reactions are uncommon; tell your vet about any previous reaction — it changes the plan, not necessarily the protection.

The senior vaccine checklist for your vet visit

  • When were core vaccines last given, and are titres worth checking?
  • Is rabies current under your local law?
  • Which non-core vaccines still fit my dog's actual lifestyle?
  • Is my dog well enough today, or should we split/defer?

Vaccination in old age is not about ticking a calendar — it is about protecting a dog whose immune system can no longer afford a serious infection, with the smallest intervention that does the job.
