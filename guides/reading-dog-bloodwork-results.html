How to Read Your Senior Dog's Bloodwork Results (Without Panicking)

Every senior dog owner has had the same experience: the vet hands over a bloodwork report covered in numbers, a few flagged in red, and your stomach drops. Here is the plain-English guide to what those numbers mean — and, just as important, what they don't.

The short version: Bloodwork is read in patterns and trends, not single numbers. One mildly high value in an otherwise healthy dog is usually watched, not treated. The values that matter most in seniors: kidney markers (BUN, creatinine, SDMA), liver enzymes (ALT, ALP), glucose, proteins (albumin, globulin), blood cells (CBC) and thyroid (T4) — always interpreted alongside the dog in front of the vet.

The kidney values

  • Creatinine & BUN (urea): the classic kidney markers. They rise when kidney function is substantially reduced — so a rise matters, but the trend over visits matters more than one reading. Dehydration alone can raise both, which is why your vet rechecks after fluids.
  • SDMA: rises earlier than creatinine (often when ~40% of function is lost rather than ~75%). A rising SDMA with normal creatinine is an early-warning conversation, not an emergency.
  • Urine concentration (from the urinalysis, always pair it with bloods): dilute urine plus rising kidney values is a stronger signal than either alone.

The liver values

  • ALT: liver cell injury — rises with liver disease, but also with many medicines and transient insults. Mild, isolated ALT bumps are commonly rechecked in a few weeks.
  • ALP: very commonly raised in older dogs, including from benign causes (steroids, bone activity, certain breeds like Schnauzers and Terriers). A high ALP alone, in a well dog, is rarely the emergency it looks like.
  • Albumin & bilirubin: low albumin or high bilirubin are taken more seriously — they suggest the liver is struggling to do its job, or protein is being lost elsewhere.

The rest of the panel

  • Glucose: high glucose plus drinking/urinating more points toward diabetes; a single high reading in a stressed dog at the clinic is often repeated before any diagnosis.
  • CBC (red cells, white cells, platelets): low red cells = anaemia (many causes, from chronic disease to bleeding); high white cells suggest infection or inflammation; low platelets need prompt attention.
  • T4 (thyroid): low T4 with weight gain, dull coat and low energy supports hypothyroidism — one of the most treatable senior diagnoses.
  • Electrolytes (sodium, potassium, calcium): calcium, in particular, is worth noting: persistently high calcium in an older dog is a reason to look for cancer, and your vet will chase it.

How to use any report

  • Ask three questions: (1) What changed since last time? (2) Which values worry you, and which are just "watch"? (3) What do we recheck, and when?
  • Keep every report. A value creeping upward inside the "normal range" over two years is often the earliest disease signal of all.
  • Don't Google single values at 2 a.m. Reference ranges vary by lab, age and breed; context is the diagnosis.

Bloodwork is a map, not a verdict. The best owners bring it to every vet visit, ask about the trends — and let the vet read the whole map, not just the red flags.
