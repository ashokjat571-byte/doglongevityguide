How Long Do Dogs Really Live? Average Lifespans by Size and Breed

"How long do dogs live?" is the question behind this entire website. The honest answer is a range: most dogs live 10 to 13 years — but size and breed move that number so much that a single average is almost meaningless. A Chihuahua and a Great Dane are the same species with nearly a decade between their expected lifespans.

The short version: Small breeds typically live 13–16+ years, medium breeds 11–14, large breeds 9–12, and giant breeds just 7–10. Within any breed, weight, dental care, preventive vet care and early disease detection are the levers owners control.

Average lifespan by size

  • Toy & small breeds (Chihuahua, Toy Poodle, Jack Russell Terrier): commonly 13–16 years, many reaching 17–18.
  • Medium breeds (Border Collie, Cocker Spaniel, Beagle): typically 11–14 years.
  • Large breeds (Labrador, Golden Retriever, German Shepherd): typically 9–12 years.
  • Giant breeds (Great Dane, Bernese Mountain Dog, Mastiff): typically 6–10 years — the Bernese is among the shortest-lived at around 6–8.

Our full breed longevity database on the homepage lists 23 popular breeds with their typical ranges and what to watch for in each.

Why size matters so much

Large breeds grow faster, driven by higher levels of the growth hormone IGF-1, and they age faster as adults — earlier cancers, earlier joint disease, earlier senior status. Small dogs, with lower IGF-1, run the same program more slowly. It is the central puzzle of canine aging, and now the target of the first lifespan drugs in development.

What owners can actually change

  • Body weight. The strongest evidence in the field: lean Labradors lived a median 1.8 years longer than their overfed siblings in a 14-year lifetime study.
  • Dental health. Periodontal disease is linked with heart, kidney and liver problems; dogs whose teeth are cared for avoid a constant source of inflammation.
  • Preventive vet care. Vaccination, parasite control and twice-yearly senior checks catch disease while it is still cheap and kind to treat.
  • Spaying/neutering is associated with longer life in large studies (in one US analysis, ~26% longer for spayed females and ~14% for neutered males), alongside individual risks your vet can weigh for your breed.
  • Daily movement and mental work — active dogs in the Dog Aging Project data had better cognitive scores in old age.

The real goal

Averages describe populations, not your dog. Plenty of Labradors reach 14 and plenty of small dogs are lost early to preventable disease. The point of knowing the numbers is not to accept them — it is to find the gap between average and possible, and close it with the levers above.
