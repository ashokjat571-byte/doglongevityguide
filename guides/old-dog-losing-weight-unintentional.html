Why Is My Old Dog Losing Weight? Causes You Shouldn't Ignore

A senior dog who is losing weight without trying is telling you something. Unlike planned weight loss (which helps overweight seniors enormously), unplanned loss — especially with muscle wasting over the spine and hips — is one of the most important warning signs in old-dog medicine. It is never explained by age alone.

Key takeaways:
  • Unplanned weight loss in a senior dog always deserves a vet visit — sooner if appetite is also down, vomiting or diarrhea are present, or the loss is fast.
  • Top causes: dental pain, kidney disease, cancer, thyroid disease, heart disease, gut disease and uncontrolled diabetes.
  • Weigh monthly at home; a 5% loss is worth mentioning, 10% is urgent.
  • “Eating more but losing weight” points in a different direction (diabetes, gut malabsorption, thyroid) than “not eating” (pain, nausea, mouth disease).

First: is it fat loss, muscle loss, or both?

Run your hands over ribs, spine and hips. Fat loss with preserved muscle suggests not eating enough or burning more (hyperthyroidism is rare in dogs; diabetes and gut disease are common). Muscle wasting with a bony spine and hips — even if the belly looks normal — suggests chronic disease, cancer or protein loss. Photograph your dog monthly from above and the side; gradual change is invisible day to day.

The most common causes, in order of how vets think

  • Mouth pain: dental disease makes eating hurt; dogs eat less, drop food, or prefer soft food. Often the first thing to check — and very fixable.
  • Kidney disease: nausea, reduced appetite, increased drinking and urination, and slow weight loss. Bloodwork (creatinine, SDMA) and urinalysis confirm it.
  • Cancer: many cancers cause weight and muscle loss before any lump is found. Unexplained loss in an old dog is a reason to look, not to assume.
  • Gut disease: inflammatory bowel disease, pancreatic insufficiency (large, pale, greasy stools) and chronic infections reduce absorption — dogs may eat ravenously and still lose weight.
  • Heart disease and diabetes: both increase energy demands and reduce appetite or nutrient use; diabetes classically causes weight loss despite a big appetite and big thirst.

What your vet will do

Expect: a full exam including mouth and lymph nodes, weight history, bloodwork (CBC, chemistry, thyroid), urinalysis, fecal test, and often chest and abdominal X-rays or ultrasound. This is a workup with a high yield — most causes are found, and many are treatable, especially dental disease and organ disease caught early.

Frequently asked questions

My old dog eats fine but is getting thinner. Is that normal?

No — it is a classic sign of malabsorption, diabetes or cancer, and it needs investigation even though appetite looks good. “Eating well” does not rule out disease.

Should I just feed more?

Tempting, but feeding more without a diagnosis can worsen some conditions (and mask the trend your vet needs to see). Small, frequent, palatable meals are reasonable while you wait for the appointment — days, not weeks.

How fast is too fast?

Any loss you can see over 2–4 weeks, or more than 10% of body weight overall, should be seen promptly. In a 20 kg dog, 2 kg is 10% — that is a lot, and it is easy to miss under a fluffy coat.

Weight is the cheapest health test you own: a scale, once a month, and a note in your phone. In a senior dog, that habit catches more disease early than almost any other.
