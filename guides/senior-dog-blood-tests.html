7 Blood Tests That Matter Most for a Senior Dog

The single highest-value habit in senior dog care is not a supplement — it is regular bloodwork. Most diseases that shorten a dog's life (kidney disease, liver disease, thyroid disorders, diabetes) are manageable when caught early and emergencies when caught late. Here are the tests that matter most after your dog turns senior — around age 7 for large breeds, 9–10 for small ones.

The short version: A good senior panel combines a complete blood count (CBC), a chemistry panel (kidneys, liver, glucose, proteins), SDMA for early kidney detection, a thyroid test (T4), and a urinalysis. Most vets recommend it once a year for young seniors and every 6 months for older or unwell seniors.

1. Complete blood count (CBC)

Counts red cells, white cells and platelets. It flags anemia, infection, inflammation and some cancers — the background health picture everything else is read against.

2. Chemistry panel — kidney values (BUN & creatinine)

The classic kidney markers. They typically rise only after substantial kidney function is lost, which is exactly why test 3 exists.

3. SDMA

SDMA rises earlier than creatinine as kidney function declines — often when only around 40% of function is lost, instead of ~75%. For a disease as common and as manageable-early as chronic kidney disease, that head start is valuable.

4. Liver enzymes (ALT, ALP, bilirubin, albumin)

The liver panel spots liver disease, gallbladder problems and side effects of long-term medicines such as arthritis painkillers — one reason bloodwork is repeated in dogs on chronic medication.

5. Blood glucose (and fructosamine if high)

Screens for diabetes, which is increasingly common in older, overweight dogs and responds well to early management.

6. Thyroid — total T4

Low thyroid (hypothyroidism) is a classic, very treatable cause of weight gain, dull coat and low energy in middle-aged and senior dogs. A screening T4 belongs on every senior panel.

7. Urinalysis

Technically not a blood test — and routinely skipped, which is a mistake. Urine concentration is one of the earliest kidney signals, and the sample also reveals infection, protein loss, glucose and crystals. Always pair it with the blood panel.

How to use the results

  • Keep every report. A value inside the "normal range" that climbs year after year is information; trends beat snapshots.
  • Ask "what changed since last time?" — it is the most useful question in senior care.
  • Establish a baseline while your dog is healthy, ideally by middle age, so later changes are obvious.

Bloodwork doesn't extend life by itself. It buys the one thing that does: time to act early.
