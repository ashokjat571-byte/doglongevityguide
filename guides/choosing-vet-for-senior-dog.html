How to Choose the Right Vet for Your Senior Dog

When your dog is young, almost any good vet will do. When your dog is old, the vet matters enormously — because senior care is mostly judgment: when to test, when to treat pain, when to operate, when to stop. Choosing the right practice is one of the highest-leverage decisions a senior owner makes.

Key takeaways:
  • Look for: senior-specific protocols (twice-yearly visits, senior blood panels), pain-management expertise, in-house diagnostics, and vets who listen to owners' observations.
  • Ask directly: how do you assess pain in old dogs? What is your anesthesia protocol for seniors? When do you refer to specialists?
  • Continuity beats convenience: a vet who knows your dog's baseline catches change faster than a new face each visit.
  • Trust is measurable: do they explain options with costs, respect your budget, and talk about quality of life honestly?

What a senior-ready practice looks like

  • Senior protocols: twice-yearly wellness visits from age 7–8, senior blood panels (CBC, chemistry with SDMA, thyroid, urinalysis), blood-pressure checks, and dental assessments as routine — not as extras you must request.
  • Pain expertise: modern pain management is multi-modal (NSAIDs, gabapentin, Adequan, laser/physio referrals where appropriate). A practice that treats pain as optional is behind the times.
  • Diagnostics on site: in-house bloodwork, X-ray and ultrasound access speed up senior decisions enormously.
  • Specialist network: good general practices refer readily — to surgery, oncology, cardiology, dentistry — rather than handling everything in-house.

Questions that reveal quality

  • “How do you decide whether my old dog's slowing down is pain or just age?” (Good answer: exam, pain trial, gait assessment — never “just age”.)
  • “What monitoring do you use for senior anesthesia?” (Good answer: full monitoring, fluids, warming, dedicated nurse.)
  • “How do you handle end-of-life discussions?” (Good answer: honestly, early, with quality-of-life scales and home options.)
  • “Can I email updates between visits?” (Continuity and communication are senior care.)

Red flags

  • Dismissing your observations (“he's just old”) without examination.
  • Recommending major procedures without bloodwork in a senior.
  • No pain plan after surgery or dental work.
  • Refusal to discuss costs or alternatives, or pressure toward a single expensive option.

Frequently asked questions

Should I switch vets when my dog becomes a senior?

Not automatically — continuity has real value. Switch if your current practice lacks senior protocols, dismisses pain, or you have lost trust. When switching, bring all records and a written history; the new vet's first senior visit should be longer, not rushed.

Are vet schools or referral hospitals better for seniors?

For complex cases (cancer, heart disease, complicated surgery), referral hospitals offer specialists and equipment general practices cannot. For day-to-day senior care, a good local practice with specialist links is usually the best base.

How do I manage costs?

Ask for estimates in writing, prioritise by what changes outcomes (diagnostics before supplements), and discuss staged plans. Pet insurance rarely starts for very old dogs, so a monthly savings pot is the realistic senior plan.

The right vet for a senior dog is a partner, not a vendor. Find the one who sees your old dog as a patient with a future, not a problem with an age — and keep them.
